PROJECT EXPLAINED IN PLAIN ENGLISH
====================================

WHAT THE ORIGINAL PAPER DID
----------------------------
Three Stanford students wanted to know: are Chinese people's social media
posts about COVID vaccines mostly positive or negative, and does that change
over time? They grabbed ~60,000 posts from Weibo (China's Twitter), hand-
labelled a few thousand as "positive" or "negative," and tried 5 different
ways of teaching a computer to guess the sentiment of a new post
automatically. They then used the best 2 methods to scan all remaining
posts and plot how opinion shifted from December 2020 to April 2021.

WHAT "REPLICATE" MEANS HERE
-----------------------------
We don't have their original labelled posts -- never published. So we
grabbed a public Weibo dataset of similar size and shape (same split sizes:
2100 to train, 850 to tune, 850 to test) and rebuilt every method the paper
describes, using their exact settings, to see if we get similar patterns.

THE 5 METHODS, IN PLAIN TERMS
--------------------------------
1. Naive Bayes -- count which words show up more in positive vs negative
   posts, multiply those odds together for a new post. Simplest, fastest,
   baseline.

2. Bigram language model -- like Naive Bayes but looks at word pairs in
   sequence instead of words alone.

3. SVM -- draws the best possible dividing line between "positive" and
   "negative" examples in a mathematical space built from the words.

4. Logistic Regression -- similar idea to SVM, gives a probability instead
   of a hard yes/no.

5. LSTM -- a neural network that reads the post word-by-word, remembering
   context as it goes, like how a person reads a sentence.

Each method was also tried with 4 different ways of chopping text into
pieces (single characters, pairs, triples, or real words via a Chinese
word-segmenter called jieba) -- because Chinese has no spaces between
words, so "how you cut it up" matters a lot.

WHAT WE FOUND
---------------
- All 5 methods land in a similar accuracy range (roughly 55-66% correct
  on data they haven't seen) -- not amazing, but matches the paper's own
  struggle; sentiment in short, sarcastic social posts is hard.
- Some methods (SVM, LSTM with fine-grained character splitting) memorize
  the training posts almost perfectly but then flounder on new ones --
  classic overfitting, same pattern the paper saw.
- Using whole words (jieba) generally works better than raw character
  slicing for the simpler methods -- matches the paper's conclusion.

WHAT WE COULDN'T DO
----------------------
The paper's final step -- tracking how opinion changed month by month as
vaccine doses rolled out -- needs posts with dates attached. Our substitute
dataset has no dates, so that part is marked as "not possible with this
data" rather than faked.

TUNING (DONE)
---------------
We then adjusted each method's internal knobs (how much to penalize
mistakes, how big the neural network is, etc.) using only the "tuning"
portion of data, and only looked at the final test data once, with the
winning settings, at the very end.

Result: Naive Bayes improved a little (less aggressive smoothing fit this
data better). The other four methods (SVM, Logistic Regression, Bigram
Model, LSTM) were already close to their best possible settings even using
the paper's original numbers -- tuning barely moved them. That itself is a
useful finding: the paper's original settings weren't far off for this
dataset.
